In Defense Of: The Manic Pixie Dream Girl

"Phaedra" by Alexandre Cabanel

I have not lived long, but I have lived long enough to love and to hate. And this is most prominent in my love-hate relationship with the concept of a manic pixie dream girl.

If you have been on the internet long enough, you have surely seen the rise and fall of the popularity of this term. A manic pixie dream girl is the type of female character that suddenly appears in the male protagonist's life, sweeps him off his feet, changes his worldview completely, and then dips forever.

Usually, I am not the one to defend clichés, but this character has been quite misunderstood by the general public.

The manic pixie is not a heartless bitch, she is just a person who has found herself and is not willing to change it. This is also why most of these characters have their quirks, ranging from the color-changing hair of Ramona Flowers in Scott Pilgrim vs. the World to the countless stacks of books of Alaska Young in Looking for Alaska.

Unlike the male characters, these women know who they are and who they aren't. They probably spent years finding their own selves prior, but that journey is almost never pictured – they always come in with already-established personalities.

They are always awful love interests, because most people think that love is some kind of sacrifice, conformity to the partner's deepest desires.

Oh, you like someone who listens to rock? Well, I will listen to rock too, even though I completely hate it!

This is sadly not how life works with manic pixie dream girls, they will not change themselves for anyone.

The male love interest simply doesn't want to regard the pixie as a living and breathing person – they rather regard her as some liquid that would take on the shape of the container it will be kept in. Why this specific liquid? Oh, it's just that they liked the color of it.

The male protagonist sees a beautiful and unique individual and that is what attracts him. Her strong sense of self is inspiring to him and he is blown away. He tries to get her no matter what, sacrificing parts of himself, but he forgets that she won't do the same. The male protagonist subconsciously wants to be the first one to "tame" and change her. Not only that, but he expects her to change.

Oh, you don't want kids? You'll change your mind.

Love is not conformity. It is the acceptance of the differences that make you who you are. The manic pixie dream girl does not regard a relationship like this as love and she knows that the male protagonist does not truly love her if he cannot accept the fact that she doesn't want to change. The thing is that she definitely can change, not because you want her to, but because she does.
